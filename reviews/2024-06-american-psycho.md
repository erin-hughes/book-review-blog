## Intro

I've talked quite a lot on this blog about how, even though I've always been drawn to supernatural and fantasy fiction, I have only been a fan of outright horror for a handful of years. [My piece on Stephen King's _The Shining_](/reviews/the-shining), for example, turned out to be part-review, part-dissection of what my newfound appreciation of the genre says about what the Covid-19 pandemic did to my psyche. But oddly enough, I've always loved Mary Harron's iconic 2000 adaptation of _American Psycho_.

Being a fan of this movie isn't exactly controversial, at least not now in 2025. Christian Bale as Patrick Bateman, the titular psycho, is one of the most iconic slasher villains in the entire genre. The movie contains so many memorable scenes, including the [infamous murder of Paul Allen](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WcavZ-nUgYM&ab_channel=ReckerHawx) as portrayed by Jared Leto, and the [hilarious business card scene](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E1nSwpxRuJo&ab_channel=ANUSREKNAK). For me, though, one of the main reasons that the movie embedded itself in my head the way that it did was because of a comment made by the original novel's author, Bret Easton Ellis, while he was reflecting on his work and its relevance to the 21st century.

[Writing in Town & Country](https://www.townandcountrymag.com/leisure/arts-and-culture/a4954/american-psycho-today/), Ellis discusses the intentions he had when creating Patrick Bateman and telling his story. He explains that the character was a manifestation of his own feelings of isolation and emptiness at that time, and it was largely by accident that he had given life to an avatar for an entire generation. On what Bateman would be doing if had lived today instead of in the late 80s, Ellis muses that he might have ended up a tech bro in Silicon Valley rather than an investment banker on Wall Street. He writes:

<articlequote author="Bret Easton Ellis" byline="American Psycho Author Bret Easton Ellis Tells Us Where Patrick Bateman Would Be Today">Sometimes I think that if I had written the book in the past decade, perhaps Bateman would have been working in Silicon Valley, living in Cupertino with excursions into San Francisco or down to Big Sur to the Post Ranch Inn and palling around with Zuckerberg and dining at the French Laundry, or lunching with Reed Hastings at Manresa in Los Gatos, wearing a Yeezy hoodie and teasing girls on Tinder.</articlequote>

I've been a professional software engineer for about seven years at the time I'm writing this, and this scenario really resonated with me, because I've worked with Patrick Batemans before. Multiple times in my career I've been confronted with a man impeccably dressed in designer labels, accessorized with a perfect smile that didn't quite meet his eyes. A person who somehow simultaneously radiated disdain for everyone in the room he didn't consider an equal (which invariably included me), and yet had this desperate, almost pathetic need to impress. Fortunately I primarily encountered these men when I was much younger, and since then I have worked with many talented developers who were much more authentic people. But I've always been struck by what an astute observation Ellis made with his comments; by how accurately he pinpointed where the pretentious hollowness of the 80s has migrated to 40 years later. That, combined with my existing love of the 2000 movie, is what led me to give the Ellis' novel a shot.

## Book vs Movie

I had a little trouble figuring out the angle that I wanted to approach an analysis of _American Psycho_ from. All of the reviews I've written so far have been about books that I had just read for the first time, and so were, for the most part, uninhibited by preconceptions or comparisons to the same story in other mediums. In this case, because of my existing relationship with the movie, it would feel a little dishonest to start discussing whether or not I like Ellis' story, because it's already something that I hold fairly dearly. Additionally, the movie adaptation of the novel is pretty faithful in terms of that core story. The main differences between the two texts, other than some name changes, are the themes that they focus on, and the tone with which they communicate those themes. The movie leans much further into the comedy side of the Wall Street satire than the book does, making it much clearer that at least some of what the audience sees play out on screen is a joke at the expense of Bateman and the yuppie culture he embodies. Ellis' tone in the novel, however, is often very apathetic and hollow, mirroring the shallowness of that culture and highlighting how empty Bateman is having dedicated his life to it.

Both texts critique the same thing, but do it in different ways. And it's those differences that I think are interesting here. So instead of litigating which version of _American Psycho_ is “better”, I'd rather take a look at some of the key themes and motifs in the story, examine how Ellis and Harron handle them, and discuss how that impacts the overall “message” that comes across at the end.

## Violence

What shocked me the most about the novel is how much more explicitly violent it is when compared to the movie. That probably sounds a bit foolish, given that the word “psycho” is literally in the title. I myself described Patrick Bateman as a slasher villain at the beginning of this review, so you would think that violence would not have been surprising to me. But what I didn't realise was just how much the movie protects the viewer from the most disturbing of Bateman's crimes.

In the book, the reader suffers through at least a dozen of Bateman's murders, each one explained in excruciating detail. His victims include sex workers, homeless people, animals, and one of his ex-girlfriends. He even murders a child in Central Park Zoo in broad daylight, managing to stab him in the throat unnoticed before disappearing back into the crowd.

Bateman also uses a sickening variety of tools and weapons throughout the book, including an axe, a nail gun, battery acid, coat hangers, power drills, and, worst of all, a starving rat. The scene involving the rat is especially nauseating (I'm being as vague as possible so as to not inflict it upon any unsuspecting readers), with even Guinevere Turner, the movie's co-writer, [claiming](https://www.dazeddigital.com/artsandculture/article/20751/1/how-american-psycho-became-a-feminist-statement) that when she first read it she thought to herself:

<articlequote author="Guinevere Turner" byline="How American Psycho became a feminist statement">Fuck you Bret Ellis, that's never gonna get out of my vision!</articlequote>

The movie mercifully chooses not to commit the majority of those more gruesome crimes to film. The audience does still see Bateman kill a handful of people, but generally the movie tastefully cuts away just before any explicit violence can be seen. For example, after Bateman's threesome with sex workers Christie and Sabrina, we only get to see him ominously open a drawer full of knives and other sharp tools before the camera cuts away. The next we see these women they are freshly wounded and fleeing his apartment, the torment that Bateman put them through left up to the viewer's imagination.

Initially when I read the book, I found myself yearning for this mercy, for the relief of not having to read about all the ways Bateman mutilated the women unfortunate enough to cross his path. And I do think the implied horror of what he does after the camera cuts away is really effective, especially in the Christie and Sabrina scene - the object we see Bateman holding just before the cut is a coat hanger, and the implied uses for this object are much scarier than anything that could have been shown on screen. However, the more that I've thought about this point, the more that I've accepted that the violence of the book is a necessary evil, because it is central to the message that Ellis is attempting to convey.

Patrick Bateman is a critique of the yuppie subculture prevalent throughout the 1980s. A yuppie is essentially a young professional person working in a city, which is not a pejorative in itself. But yuppies are typically ambitious, materialistic, and pretentious, and the growth of their subculture led to concerns about gentrification and displacement of existing communities by the influx of these affluent young people.

There is definitely an ugliness to the stereotypical yuppie - a person unshakeably dedicated to their career, obsessed with the latest trends, and judgemental of people less well off than them. And if you take that stereotype to the ugliest, most violent place possible, you end up with Patrick Bateman. A man who murders his colleagues because he's jealous of their business cards or the projects they're working on. A man who mindlessly parrots reviews of trendy restaurants and albums to the women he rapes and slaughters, because to Ellis, Bateman's desperation to fit in and be accepted by the society he lives in is as dangerous as his bloodlust. This is why the violence is portrayed in such an extreme way - it's a meta-commentary on how destructive the very idea of Patrick Bateman is. He and his culture are grotesque, so therefore the way in which Ellis presents his critiques is also grotesque.

That said, of course, understanding the purpose of the explicit tortures and murders doesn't make them any easier for the reader to endure. Their presence makes the novel less accessible to more casual or squeamish readers. And this is where the movie's approach really works. The audience still understands that Bateman is a killer, without having to endure the specifics of him actually carrying out his killings. They might not be experiencing Ellis' undiluted vision, but they do still get to experience the spirit of it, when they otherwise might have turned away. So, which should we consider "effective" - an uncompromising, unabashedly violent book that readers might not be able to stomach; or a comparably watered down adaptation that is more palatable to a casual audience?

## Identity

In the novel, Patrick Bateman is constantly mistaken for someone else and called by other names. Paul Allen (called Paul Owen in the book) confuses Bateman with another colleague and asks him about that man's girlfriend, Bateman's lawyer mistakes him for a man called Davis, and someone that Bateman himself has trouble placing calls him Ted Owen. It also happen frequently to his colleagues and the others in their yuppie sphere, and they even mix up the names of restaurants and bars that they frequent - for example, "Barcadia" and "Arcadia" are two different names that seem to be used interchangeably to refer to the same venue.

This is a thread that persists the whole way through the novel. The reader is constantly reminded that no-one seems to really know who Bateman is, and he doesn't seem entirely certain about the identities of his peers either. In the movie, Harron dedicates an entire scene - [the infamous business card scene](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E1nSwpxRuJo&ab_channel=ANUSREKNAK) - to this idea, and it is one of the most memorable and enduring moments of the entire thing.

It begins with Bateman complaining that Paul Allen always mixes him up with his colleague, Marcus Halberstram. It's a comparison that infuriates Bateman, but one that he concedes is understandable, since both men have the same job, wear similar suits, similar glasses, and go to the same barber. The scene then devolves into what is ostensibly a dick measuring contest, where Bateman and his colleagues compare their business cards and bicker over who has the best one, despite them all looking comically similar.

This is the clearest example in the movie of how interchangeable all of these men ultimately are. They're always competing with each other to be the best in their careers, which means that they need to have the best business cards, the best suits, and the best projects to work on (like the Fisher account like Paul Allen is managing). Since they're all in agreement of what the "best" is, the pursuit of perfection means that they all end up almost indistinguishable. Their desire for power comes at the expense of their individuality, resulting in the homogenous yuppie subculture that Bateman represents.

It's a great scene, but it's one that's played mostly for laughs. The audience is expected to find Bateman's total despair at Paul Allen having a "better" business card than him comical. The same moment was satirical in the novel as well, but I feel like the movie equivalent didn't quite capture how empty and vapid these men really are.

Mistaken identities as a theme never really comes up again in the movie. It only really appears during Bateman's encounters with Paull Allen, and as such it comes across like it's a mistake that only Allen makes - a mistake that ultimately allows Bateman to get away with murdering him, because mixing him up with Marcus Halberstram gives him an alibi. In the book, it's much clearer that this is a problem that everyone in Bateman's social circle comes up against, and not just the mistake of one man. As such, it works better as a critique of the entire yuppie subculture; the movie focuses on mocking Bateman as an individual.

## Impotence

One of the things that made the movie adaptation of _American Psycho_ so enduring is Christian Bale's performance as Patrick Bateman. On page, Bateman is such an insane character, oscillating wildly between spewing generic music review to murdering women with nail guns to holding back tears at the thought of having to wait for a table in a restaurant. Bale captures all of this effortlessly, and his performance is as wacky as it is chilling. One thing that I never would have guessed about Bateman from Bale's portrayal, however, is how often he talks about sexual impotence.

I imagine that this will surprise other movie-only fans of _American Psycho_, because there's absolutely no indication in the movie that Bateman suffers from performance issues. When we see him having sex with women his partners seem satisfied and happy (up until the bloodlust takes over and he starts murdering them, of course). Bateman himself seems relatively happy too, flexing his muscles and winking at himself in the mirror throughout the encounters. Bale achieved an incredible physique for this role, and he looks every inch the sexually powerful alpha male that Bateman wants to be seen as. Performance issues or lack of interest are not hinted at whatsoever.

The book tells a different story. Sex scenes are described clinically and in a disconnected way. Bateman rhymes off the acts he does with his partners like he's filling in a checklist, stating very matter-of-factly “sex happens - a hardcore montage”. He never describes the experience with any heat or passion, or even pride that he got someone to do those things with him.
Impotence is a regular occurrence for Bateman in the novel. He describes watching two female sex workers that he hired touch each other, but soon says that “it starts failing to turn [him] on”. Elsewhere he describes “weak” orgasms and talks about how his erections “wilt”. It's passive, sad language like that that paints the book version of Bateman as a much more pathetic sexual figure than he appears in the movie.

Performance issues like this don't inherently make someone lesser, in the same way that sexual prowess doesn't make someone a good person. But this thread does help to highlight how much of Bateman's presentation is pure facade. He's not capable of the feats that he wants everyone to believe he is. And that inability to conform to what culture dictates he should be is what causes him to lash out at the world. The movie portrays a fairly stereotypical alpha male who uses women as tools for his own pleasure. That's still accurate to Bateman in the book, but there's an added nuance there that I feel is lost on screen. It could be that Bale is just too charming and sexy to even act sexually impotent, but regardless, his portrayal does miss that particular aspect of Bateman's pathos.

## Conclusion

Despite my 4 star rating for the _American Psycho_ novel, it's not a book that I find easy to recommend. Ellis' book is uncompromising and violent. It takes no prisoners, and at times it felt like it was almost daring me to offended, like somehow it would win if I found its content too much and put it down.

The movie is a much easier entry point to Ellis' critiques. It takes the core of the message - that the pressures of our society can twist people into unrecognisable monsters - and distills it into truly brilliant comedy thriller. However, that distillation process is not without cost. There are nuances to Ellis' work that don't quite survive the adaptation - its confrontational tone, the transience of its characters' identities, the pathos of the true man that lurks beneath the yippie veneer that Patrick Bateman presents to the world.

No adaptation is perfect. A story will always change when migrating from one medium to another, if for other reason than ensuring it still makes sense within that medium. What Harron has done with her 2000 movie is genuinely impressive. She managed to take one of the most controversial novels of the 20th century and produce a story about a slasher villain that's just as much of a biting satire, and yet, much more accessible for people to experience.

I do not regret reading _American Psycho_. But I'm not sure I would have survived it if I had not already fallen in love with the movie. And in comparing both, I've found that, while I don't think it's useful to declare one version "better" than the other, I do think the order in which you experience them might help. The movie primed me for the novel; it piqued my interest in what Ellis had to say, and that carried me through the most abrasive passages. It enabled me to enjoy it.

So while I can't honestly recommend the novel on its own, I do recommend giving the movie a shot if you haven't already seen it. I  hope it'll capture your imagination the way it captured mine. And if it does, maybe we can trade pretentious reviews about it, just like Patrick Bateman would want.
